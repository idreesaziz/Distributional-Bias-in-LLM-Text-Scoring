% ABSTRACT
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\begin{abstract}
LLMs asked to rate text quality systematically avoid extremes,
compressing responses into a narrow interior band despite true quality
variation. We measure this \emph{score compression} across eleven
models (3.8B--228B parameters) with $\sim$99{,}000 evaluations of 150
expert-authored articles degraded along four axes (grammar, coherence,
information, lexical sophistication) at five controlled intensities.
Compression ratios range from 0.026 (Mistral~7B) to 0.685
(GPT-5~mini), with a positive size--compression correlation among nine
open-weight models (Pearson $r = 0.78$, $p = 0.013$). Neither
GPT-5~mini nor Gemini~3~Flash assigns 0 or 10 across 9{,}000 samples
each---a pattern shared by four additional models---despite simulations
predicting hundreds of such scores. A three-way mixed-effects model
confirms significant main effects and all interactions. Across five
mitigations on a nine-model comparison, auxiliary regression gives the
largest pairwise-accuracy gain ($0.518 \to 0.702$), expected-score
rescaling improves rank correlation ($\rho\colon 0.458 \to 0.490$),
quantile normalization restores the full scale while preserving rank
order, and contrastive anchor prompting is mixed. Post-hoc affine
calibration reduces RMSE by 10--26\%, confirming a systematic,
partially correctable distortion with direct implications for any use
of LLM-generated quality ratings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLMs are increasingly used as automated text evaluators---grading
essays, rating content quality, scoring summaries, and benchmarking
generated text~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval,kocmi2023large}---under
the assumption that they map quality to numeric scales with reasonable
fidelity~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}. Yet they systematically
avoid rating extremes~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}: pristine,
publication-quality text receives 7 or 8 rather than 9 or 10, while
heavily corrupted text rarely falls below 2 or 3 (Table~\ref{tab:dist};
Figure~\ref{fig:histograms}). This \emph{score compression} has direct
consequences: compressing $[0,10]$ to roughly $[2,9]$ reduces
resolution by at least 30\%~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval},
degrades ranking reliability (Spearman $\rho$ as low as 0.138;
Table~\ref{tab:compression}), and miscalibrates threshold decisions
(\eg ``pass if score $\geq 7$''; Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

Under a naive linear model, text degraded to fraction $\lambda$ of its
original quality should score $S = 10(1 - \lambda)$. Instead, GPT-5~mini
follows $S \approx 8 - 6.5\lambda$ and Gemini~3~Flash follows $S
\approx 8 - 6.2\lambda$ (Section~\ref{sec:results},
Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}): the intercept is below 10, the slope is
only $\sim$65\% of ideal (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}), and the
usable range shrinks by roughly one third.

We call this under-utilisation of numeric rating scales \emph{Distributional
Bias}. Prior work has catalogued \emph{selection} and \emph{categorical}
biases in LLM judges, including position
bias~\citep{zheng2023judging,shi2024judging} and verbosity
bias~\citep{zheng2023judging}. Those biases explain \emph{which} item a
model prefers~\citep{wang2024large}; distributional bias describes
\emph{how} the model uses the quantitative scale to express that
preference, independent of the item being evaluated---a dimension absent
from existing bias taxonomies~\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024large,liu2023geval}.
\citet{zheng2023judging} note scale-use tendencies but do not quantify
them; \citet{liu2023geval} observe miscalibration and propose a partial
remedy without systematic cross-model replication; \citet{wang2024large}
document ranking distortions without measuring compression magnitude.
No prior work has quantified compression against an algebraically
defined quality gradient, replicated it across eleven models spanning
providers and scales, or systematically evaluated mitigations.

Whether distributional bias stems from RLHF training
dynamics~\citep{rafailov2023direct}, the mode-seeking property of
reverse KL-divergence~\citep{rafailov2023direct}, or pretraining
frequency effects remains mechanistically open; the phenomenon itself is
empirically tractable and reproduced across eleven diverse models
(Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}). We measure it with three design features:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Metamorphic behavioral testing~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond} with a known ground-truth gradient.}
    Metamorphic testing probes models through semantically meaningful
    transformations with known expected output directions, without
    requiring ground-truth labels~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}. Rather than
    compare LLM judgments with noisy human annotations---which include
    inter-rater disagreement and central-tendency
    biases~\citep{stevens1971issues}---we use deterministic,
    algorithmically defined degradations to build a controlled quality
    gradient~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}: Level~5 is objectively worse than
    Level~1, enabling unambiguous compression measurement
    (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic_justification}).
    \item \textbf{Psychometric axis isolation~\citep{attali2006automated,mcnamara2010linguistic}.}
    Psychometric axis isolation decomposes writing quality into
    independent, theoretically grounded subscores targeting distinct
    linguistic tiers, so one dimension can be degraded without
    confounding others~\citep{attali2006automated}. Following Automated
    Essay Scoring (AES) frameworks~\citep{attali2006automated}, we
    manipulate grammar, coherence, information content, and lexical
    sophistication---surface syntax, discourse structure, semantics, and
    vocabulary~\citep{mcnamara2010linguistic}---to test whether
    compression is uniform or concentrated in specific cognitive tiers
    (Section~\ref{sec:axis_justification}).
    \item \textbf{Multi-model evaluation.} Eleven models spanning
    3.8B--228B parameters (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:models}), including frontier
    API models (GPT-5~mini, Gemini~3~Flash) and open-weight models from
    Meta, Google, Microsoft, Alibaba, Mistral~AI, and MiniMax, show that
    distributional bias is cross-architectural rather than model-specific
    (Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}).
\end{enumerate}
We declare three \emph{a priori} primary outcomes: compression ratio per
model (Section~\ref{sec:results}), average regression slope of score on
degradation level per model (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and median
paired score difference between models on identical samples
(Section~\ref{sec:intermodel}).

Beyond documenting central tendency bias in a new
modality~\citep{stevens1971issues,guilford1954psychometric}, our
contributions are: (i) formally characterising \emph{distributional
bias} and providing the first \emph{quantified} compression magnitude
against an algebraically defined ground truth, whereas prior taxonomies
omit this class or treat it as incidental~\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024large};
(ii) cross-model replication across eleven models spanning providers,
architectures, and scales (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:models}); (iii) axis-specific
sensitivity profiles revealing systematic insensitivity to
coherence-level degradation~\citep{barzilay2008modeling,lapata2005automatic}
(Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:forest}); (iv) a calibration recovery experiment in
which a simple affine transform reduces the gap by 10--26\%
(Section~\ref{sec:calibration}); and (v) a five-strategy mitigation
study showing that post-hoc corrections partially recover lost
range (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{LLM-as-a-Judge Bias Taxonomy}

Since ``LLM-as-a-judge'' frameworks emerged~\citep{zheng2023judging},
work has catalogued failures such as positional bias (favouring
candidates by order)~\citep{zheng2023judging,shi2024judging} and
verbosity bias (equating length with quality)~\citep{zheng2023judging}.
\citet{wang2024large} show that rankings are susceptible to positional
bias and that LLM judges are generally ``unfair evaluators.''
\citet{li2025preference} add ``preference leakage,'' a contamination
problem caused by relatedness between the synthetic-data generator and
the judge. These taxonomies focus on \emph{selection} and
\emph{categorical} biases---which item the model
prefers~\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024large,shi2024judging}---but do
not quantify compression against controlled ground truth or evaluate
mitigations systematically (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}).

\subsection{Central Tendency Bias: Human Rating and LLM Outputs}

Human raters avoid Likert-scale extremes, compressing distributions
around the midpoint~\citep{stevens1971issues}; expertise, scale length,
and anchoring modulate this effect~\citep{guilford1954psychometric}.
AES models trained on human ratings inherit these
properties~\citep{ke2019automated}, and traditional scoring engines use
affine scaling to recover variance~\citep{attali2006automated}. Those
studies concern models \emph{fine-tuned} on human-rating
datasets~\citep{ke2019automated}; we measure the intrinsic zero-shot
calibration of general-purpose instruct models, matching common
LLM-as-judge deployments~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}.

\subsection{Pointwise Scoring vs.\ Pairwise Ranking}

LLM judges appear stronger at relative pairwise comparison than absolute
point-wise scoring~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}. Pointwise
scoring compresses a broad distribution of possible human judgments into
one deterministic label, encouraging central defaults~\citep{liu2023geval,stevens1971issues}.
\citet{kocmi2023large} show GPT-based metrics achieve state-of-the-art
MT correlation without analyzing distributional properties; \citet{liu2023geval}
find miscalibration and propose expected-score rescaling as a partial
remedy (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}). Our result that models can have
high rank correlations (Spearman $\rho$ up to 0.80) while severe
absolute compression remains (CR as low as 0.026; Section~\ref{sec:results};
Table~\ref{tab:compression}) explains why raw scores should be treated
as ordinal rather than interval signals~\citep{stevens1971issues}.

\subsection{Mechanistic Background}

Alignment training offers a plausible compression mechanism; the full KL/RLHF
background is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:rlhf_mechanism}. The present study
focuses on documenting score compression rather than isolating its training-time
cause (Section~\ref{sec:design}).

\subsection{Synthetic Perturbation Methodology}

We audit LLM judges with a CheckList-style behavioral testing
paradigm~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}, using Directional Expectation (DIR)
perturbations in which quality degradation \emph{should} reduce scores,
rather than held-out human labels. This avoids annotator
bias~\citep{stevens1971issues} and supplies mathematical ground truth:
by construction, degradation at $\lambda = 0.8$ is objectively worse than
$\lambda = 0.2$, independent of any annotator's
judgment~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Rationale: Synthetic Perturbations vs.\ Human Baselines}
\label{sec:synthetic_justification}

Human judgment is the traditional NLP-evaluation gold standard, but it
suffers from inter-rater disagreement, fatigue, and raters'
central-tendency biases~\citep{stevens1971issues}. Accuracy tests on
human-annotated data can also overestimate model performance when
models and labels share corpus shortcuts~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}.
Following CheckList~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}, we instead use targeted,
deterministic perturbations to create a controlled quality gradient.
Thus $\lambda = 0.8$ is objectively worse than $\lambda = 0.2$ by
construction, giving mathematical ground truth for measuring compression
independent of annotator judgment~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}.

\subsection{The Four-Axis Quality Decomposition}
\label{sec:axis_justification}

Our four degradation axes follow psychometric work on text quality.
Classic AES systems such as ETS e-rater decompose writing into surface
mechanics, discourse coherence, content, and vocabulary subscores~\citep{attali2006automated};
linguistic studies likewise show that syntactic, discourse, semantic,
and lexical features independently predict writing quality~\citep{mcnamara2010linguistic}.
By isolating surface syntax (Grammar), global discourse structure
(Coherence), factual completeness (Information), and vocabulary richness
(Lexical sophistication), we test both compression magnitude and the
linguistic dimensions LLM evaluators weight most. Uniform compression
would imply a global numeric calibration failure; axis-specific patterns
would reveal qualitative sensitivity gaps (Section~\ref{sec:results};
Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:forest}). This decomposition is zero-shot and
general-purpose, unlike prior AES calibration work on models fine-tuned
on human ratings~\citep{ke2019automated}.

\subsection{Corpus Construction}

The corpus contains 150 articles from \emph{The Conversation}
(theconversation.com), where academic experts write evidence-based
articles for general audiences under professional editorial review.
Articles span Arts \& Culture, Business \& Economy, Health, Politics \&
Society, and Science \& Technology, and are kept at full length for
topical diversity. This source provides an unambiguous quality ceiling
for the $\lambda=0$ baseline: credentialed experts and editorial review
avoid the ambiguity of crowdsourced or Wikipedia corpora. The 150-article
size matches NLP metric-evaluation practice~\citep{kocmi2023large,liu2023geval},
offering statistical power under API cost constraints.

\subsection{Degradation Engine}

Each article is degraded along one orthogonal axis at level
$\lambda \in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grammar} ($G$): surface errors (typos, agreement,
    tense, article, homophone, preposition, and word-order errors)
    as categorized by~\citet{attali2006automated}, implemented by a
    nine-stage pipeline.
    \item \textbf{Coherence} ($C$): bounded random sentence-order swaps,
    preserving content and grammar. Sentence shuffling is a standard
    discourse-coherence benchmark that disrupts entity transitions and
    rhetorical structure while preserving sentence-level syntax~\citep{barzilay2008modeling,lapata2005automatic}.
    \item \textbf{Information} ($I$): progressive deletion via a
    five-phase pipeline targeting parentheticals, modifiers,
    prepositional phrases, subordinate clauses, and content words~\citep{attali2006automated}.
    \item \textbf{Lexical} ($L$): vocabulary flattening by replacing
    content words with high-frequency synonyms using embedding
    similarity, following lexical simplification methodology~\citep{glavas2015simplifying}.
\end{itemize}
At $\lambda=0$, the original text is returned. We cap intensity at
$\lambda=0.8$ rather than $1.0$ because full intensity on some axes
creates incoherent output that may trigger refusal rather than scoring.
All functions are deterministic via per-sample seeds:
$\text{MD5}(\text{title} \| \text{axis} \| \text{level} \| \text{rep}) \bmod 2^{31}$.

\subsection{Factorial Design}

The experiment uses a $150 \times 4 \times 5 \times 3$ factorial design:
\begin{equation}
    N = |\mathcal{C}| \times |\mathcal{A}| \times |\mathcal{L}| \times
    R = 150 \times 4 \times 5 \times 3 = 9{,}000
    \label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
Five stepped intensities follow synthetic perturbation practice~\citep{ribeiro2020beyond}
and allow linear and non-linear sensitivity detection. $R=3$ satisfies
the minimum for ICC(1,1) estimation (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app})
and measures intra-model variance, yielding 9{,}000 samples per model
and 99{,}000 evaluations across 11 models (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\subsection{Scoring Protocol}

Each sample is scored in isolation with: ``Rate the quality of the
following text from 0 to 10. Respond with ONLY the number.'' No rubric,
examples, or reference texts are provided; this measures intrinsic
calibration on an unanchored zero-shot scale~\citep{zheng2023judging},
as commonly deployed, leaving elaborate prompts (\eg chain-of-thought or
rubric scoring~\citep{liu2023geval}) for future work.

Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:models} lists the full model configurations.



GPT-5~mini is a reasoning model with no user-set temperature: passing
\texttt{temperature} returns \textit{``Unsupported parameter: `temperature'
is not supported with this model.''} Its sampling is therefore fixed at
an internal default.\footnote{Empirically, majority-vote aggregation over
three repetitions yields near-identical mean scores (mean absolute
deviation from single-sample mean $<0.05$), confirming that stochasticity
does not materially affect results (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app}).}
Gemini~3~Flash runs at temperature 0.0; the nine other models also use
temperature 0.0 and return token log-probabilities, enabling logprob-based
mitigations (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results are organized around the three primary outcomes, followed by
secondary analyses. All tables order models by descending compression
ratio.

\subsection{Score Distributions and Boundary Avoidance}

Most models use only a narrow subset of the 0--10 scale
(Table~\ref{tab:dist}). GPT-5~mini ($[1,9]$), Gemini~3~Flash ($[2,9]$),
Gemma~2~9B, Llama~3.1~8B, Phi-4~14B, and Qwen~2.5~14B never assign 0
or 10 across 9{,}000 samples each. Among models that do, MiniMax~M2P1
assigns 686 tens (7.7\%) and GPT-OSS~120B assigns 394 zeros (4.4\%);
Mistral~7B, Phi-4~mini, and Qwen~2.5~7B produce fewer than 25 extremes
each. Means range from 4.23 (GPT-OSS~120B) to 8.62 (Qwen~2.5~7B), with
smaller models clustering at higher means and lower SDs, consistent with
stronger compression (Table~\ref{tab:dist}; Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}).
Figure~\ref{fig:histograms} visualizes all eleven distributions.

To test whether missing extremes are a design artifact, we fit a linear
model per model, simulate 100 datasets with Gaussian noise at observed
residual SD, and count predicted boundary scores. For the six
boundary-free models, simulations predict many extremes: GPT-5~mini
should produce $\sim$542 zeros and $\sim$122 tens; Gemini~3~Flash should
produce $\sim$73 zeros and $\sim$147 tens (Table~\ref{tab:dist}). The
four other boundary-free models show the same pattern. Even models with
extremes produce far fewer than predicted (\eg Mistral: 5 tens vs.~$\sim$407 predicted), confirming active boundary avoidance
(Figure~\ref{fig:histograms}; Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:perlevel}).

\subsection{Primary Outcome 1: Compression Ratio}

Under a naive linear scorer mapping $\lambda$ to $S = 10(1 - \lambda)$,
the expected score span between $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 0.8$ is
8 points. The compression ratio is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathit{CR} = \frac{\bar{S}(\lambda = 0) - \bar{S}(\lambda =
    0.8)}{10(1 - 0) - 10(1 - 0.8)} = \frac{\bar{S}(0) -
    \bar{S}(0.8)}{8}
    \label{eq:compression}
\end{equation}
$\mathit{CR} = 1$ indicates no compression; $\mathit{CR} < 1$
indicates compression.

with CRs from 0.026 (Mistral~7B) to 0.685 (GPT-5~mini); all CIs exclude

\subsection{Primary Outcome 2: Sensitivity (Regression Slopes)}

Linear regression of score on degradation level quantifies sensitivity
(Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:forest}; full values in Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}).
Across all models, 43 of 44 slopes are significantly negative ($p<0.001$);
the exception is Mistral~7B on lexical degradation ($\beta_1=-0.03$,
$p=0.49$). Information deletion is steepest for GPT-5~mini
($\beta_1=-7.87$) and Gemini~3~Flash ($-7.05$); coherence is shallowest
for GPT-5~mini ($-4.78$), lexical for Gemini~3~Flash ($-5.26$). Mean
slopes range from $-6.46$ (GPT-5~mini) to $-0.28$ (Mistral~7B), versus
ideal $-10$; only the top API models reach two-thirds of ideal
sensitivity, and nine of eleven reach less than half.

The shallow coherence slopes are notable: random sentence shuffling
destroys argumentative flow and entity-grid coherence transitions~\citep{barzilay2008modeling}
while preserving surface features, yet models penalise it less than
comparable grammatical or informational degradation. Point-wise LLM
evaluation therefore appears to rely more on local fluency than global
discourse structure~\citep{barzilay2008modeling,lapata2005automatic}.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

Robustness analyses confirm monotonic but mildly concave score--degradation
relationships, high intra-condition reliability, large undegraded-vs.-maximally-degraded
effects, negligible length effects, significant category effects, and robustness to
Benjamini--Hochberg correction. Details, including variance diagnostics, ICCs,
effect sizes, covariate tests, category effects, and FDR results, are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:movedanalyses}, especially Appendix~\ref{app:secondary_diagnostics} and the following robustness subsections.

\subsection{Mixed-Effects Model}
\label{sec:mixedeffects}

For GPT-5~mini and Gemini~3~Flash, a three-way mixed-effects model with
article-level random intercepts and slopes confirms a large negative degradation
effect, significant axis effects, significant Level$\times$Axis$\times$Model
interactions, and non-trivial article-level random effects. Full specification and
model-selection rationale are in Appendix~\ref{app:mixedeffects_details}; coefficients are in Appendix~\ref{app:mixed}
and Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:mixedeffects_app}.

\subsection{Primary Outcome 3: Inter-Model Comparison}
\label{sec:intermodel}

Despite high agreement (Pearson $r = 0.836$, Spearman $\rho = 0.817$,
quadratic weighted $\kappa = 0.772$), Gemini~3~Flash scores
systematically higher than GPT-5~mini by a median of $+1$ point ($p <
10^{-300}$, rank-biserial $r = +0.705$). This offset is present
across all four axes: largest on grammar, information, and lexical
($+1$); smallest on coherence ($0$). All per-axis Wilcoxon tests are
significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}).

Figures~\ref{fig:scatter}--\ref{fig:paireddiff} show the agreement
scatter and paired-difference distribution.



\subsection{Calibration Recovery}
\label{sec:calibration}

We test post-hoc correctability by splitting 150 articles 80/20
(training: 120 articles, 7{,}200 samples; test: 30 articles, 1{,}800
samples) and fitting affine, four-parameter sigmoid, and isotonic
calibrations on training data.

Affine calibration reduces RMSE by 25\% for GPT-5~mini ($2.51 \to
1.88$) and 10\% for Gemini~3~Flash ($1.94 \to 1.74$)
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:calibration_app}). Sigmoid and isotonic variants
add only marginal improvement, showing that compression is approximately
linear and that residual RMSE of $\sim$1.7--1.9 points is an irreducible
floor from imperfect rank discrimination---no monotonic transform can
recover it~\citep{liu2023geval}. Rank correlations are unchanged by all
calibrations, as expected (Figure~\ref{fig:calibrecovery}). Thus affine
correction is practical and cheap, but the RMSE floor shows compression
is not merely an offset: models lose discriminative information that
rescaling cannot restore (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).


\subsection{Scaling and Generalization Across Models}
\label{sec:multimodel}

Across models from 3.8B to 228B parameters under the same minimal prompt,
CR ranges from 0.026 (Mistral~7B) to 0.685 (GPT-5~mini). Among nine
open-weight models with known sizes, $\log_{10}$ parameter count correlates
positively with CR (Pearson $r=0.78$, $p=0.013$), but non-monotonic
exceptions show that size alone is insufficient. Full scaling details are in
Appendix~\ref{app:multimodel_full}.

% 7. DISCUSSION

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The Compression Effect}

Score compression is robust across all eleven models. GPT-5~mini and
Gemini~3~Flash use only 62--69\% of the ideal range and never produce
boundary scores; CRs span 0.026--0.685 (Table~\ref{tab:compression},
Section~\ref{sec:results}). Calibrated simulations show this is not a
design artifact: equivalent linear scorers would produce hundreds of
extreme scores (Section~\ref{sec:results}; Table~\ref{tab:dist}).
Compression is asymmetric and concave: pristine texts average 7.3--8.7
rather than 10 (Figure~\ref{fig:undegraded}; Table~\ref{tab:dist}),
maximally degraded texts remain above the expected floor, and 34 of 44
axis$\times$model regressions have significant quadratic terms
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:nonlin}, Figure~\ref{fig:residuals}). Axis
sensitivity reveals a psychometric blind spot: models penalise
information loss and grammar more than discourse coherence
(Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:forest}; Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}). Sentence
shuffling destroys argumentative structure and entity transitions~\citep{barzilay2008modeling},
yet scores change less than for sentence-level perturbations, suggesting
point-wise LLM evaluation overweights local token fluency relative to
document-level reasoning~\citep{lapata2005automatic}.

\subsection{Absolute Scoring vs.\ Rank Ordering}

High rank correlations (Spearman $\rho$ up to 0.80;
Table~\ref{tab:compression}) coexist with severe absolute compression
(CR as low as 0.026; Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:multimodel}).
Models perceive the quality gradient and often order degraded texts
below intact ones, but their numeric translation is unreliable. Without
relative anchors, they default toward the centre of the learned output
distribution, consistent with reverse-KL mode seeking in RLHF~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
This matches evidence that LLMs are better pairwise rankers than absolute
scorers~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval} and explains why post-hoc
rank corrections are small ($\Delta\rho\leq0.038$;
Table~\ref{tab:mitcompare}): rank information is mostly present, but
absolute calibration is lost and no monotonic rescaling can fully recover
it (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\subsection{Axis-Specific Sensitivity}

Most models share an axis ordering: information deletion is steepest,
while coherence or lexical disruption is shallowest
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}, Figure~\ref{fig:crossaxis}), with
exceptions such as Llama~3.1~8B peaking on grammar. Low coherence
sensitivity matters because sentence shuffling destroys argumentative
flow and entity transitions~\citep{barzilay2008modeling} while preserving
surface features, again suggesting overweighting of sentence-level
quality over global discourse structure~\citep{lapata2005automatic}.

\subsection{Systematic Inter-Model Bias}

Despite high agreement ($r=0.836$; Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}),
Gemini~3~Flash scores one point higher than GPT-5~mini: a systematic
offset (rank-biserial $r=0.705$; Gemini higher on 85\% of disagreements;
Section~\ref{sec:intermodel}, Figure~\ref{fig:paireddiff}). Provider
scores are not interchangeable; a threshold calibrated on GPT will fail
systematically on Gemini (Section~\ref{sec:intermodel};
Table~\ref{tab:compression}), analogous to inter-rater scale-use
differences in human studies~\citep{guilford1954psychometric}.

Additional discussion of linearity, reliability, and calibration-related prior work
is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:discussion_details}.

\subsection{Implications}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Score calibration is necessary.} Raw LLM scores should
    not be treated as ground truth~\citep{liu2023geval,zheng2023judging};
    quantile normalization is a simple range-recovery method~\citep{bolstad2003comparison}
    (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}).
    \item \textbf{Model-specific thresholds.} A 7 from GPT-5~mini is not
    equivalent to a 7 from Gemini~3~Flash (Section~\ref{sec:intermodel};
    Figure~\ref{fig:paireddiff}).
    \item \textbf{Ordinal, not interval.} Score spacing is non-uniform~\citep{stevens1971issues};
    effective endpoints differ from nominal ones (Table~\ref{tab:dist}).
    \item \textbf{Axis awareness.} LLMs are less sensitive to
    coherence-level quality variation (Appendix~Figure~\ref{fig:forest};
    Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}).
    \item \textbf{Post-hoc corrections help modestly.} Maximum
    $\Delta\rho=+0.038$ (Table~\ref{tab:mitcompare}) shows rankings are
    largely fixed at inference time~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
    \item \textbf{Prompt-level interventions are unreliable.}
    Contrastive anchoring is inconsistent (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation})
    and not robust~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
    \item \textbf{Model scale matters noisily.} Larger models tend to
    compress less (Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}), but exceptions exist
    (Table~\ref{tab:compression}); size alone is insufficient.
\end{enumerate}

% 8. CONCLUSION

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Score compression is robust across eleven models (3.8B--228B parameters,
multiple providers) and $\sim$99{,}000 evaluations
(Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:multimodel}). CRs range from 0.026
to 0.685 and correlate positively with model size ($r=0.78$;
Table~\ref{tab:compression}, Section~\ref{sec:multimodel}), with notable
exceptions. GPT-5~mini and Gemini~3~Flash compress the effective range to
62--69\% of ideal (Section~\ref{sec:results}; Table~\ref{tab:compression}).

Across five mitigations (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}), quantile
normalization~\citep{bolstad2003comparison} restores range,
expected-score rescaling~\citep{liu2023geval} modestly improves rank
correlation, auxiliary regression gives the best pairwise-accuracy gains,
and contrastive anchor prompting~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}
is inconsistent; maximum $\Delta\rho=+0.038$ (Table~\ref{tab:mitcompare})
shows rankings are largely baked in at inference~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.

This is the first study to quantify distributional bias against an
algebraically defined ground truth across eleven models, four
psychometric axes~\citep{attali2006automated,mcnamara2010linguistic}, and
five mitigations. The title captures the finding: no one gets an A---LLMs
resist top marks and failing grades (Table~\ref{tab:dist}), funneling
scores into a safe middle range consistent with mode-seeking alignment
training~\citep{rafailov2023direct}. Correcting this bias is essential
for any application using LLM-generated quality ratings~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
% LIMITATIONS
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section*{Limitations}

First, although degradation axes are designed to be orthogonal
(Section~\ref{sec:axis_justification}), residual correlations may remain
(\eg information deletion can affect coherence~\citep{barzilay2008modeling}).
Second, the corpus is English expository text from one source; other
languages, genres, and domains require study~\citep{mcnamara2010linguistic}.
Third, the broader model landscape, especially commercial models without
logprob access, may vary further (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation};
Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:models}).

Fourth, the temperature asymmetry is a confound: GPT-5~mini does not
support user-set temperature, while Gemini~3~Flash runs at 0.0. However,
a temperature explanation predicts GPT should compress \emph{more} than
Gemini, opposite the observed CRs (0.685 vs.\ 0.620;
Table~\ref{tab:compression}). GPT's ICC remains excellent at 0.926
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app}), and majority-vote means are nearly
identical (mean absolute deviation $<0.05$). Future work should compare
matched temperatures where APIs permit.

Fifth, logprob mitigations cover only the nine models exposing token
probabilities, and contrastive prompting only two API models; controlled
matching-model comparisons address this (Table~\ref{tab:mitcompare}).
Sixth, the reference $S=10(1-\lambda)$ assumes linear quality degradation:
a mathematical convenience, not a perceptual claim. Boundary-score
absence (Section~\ref{sec:results}; Table~\ref{tab:dist}) is independent
of this assumption; under concave ground truth, our CRs would be
understated, so the benchmark is conservative.

Seventh, the minimal scoring prompt measures intrinsic calibration but
leaves chain-of-thought, decomposed evaluation, and fine-grained
rubrics~\citep{liu2023geval} for future work. The contrastive anchor
experiment (Section~\ref{sec:mitresults}) suggests the bias is not purely
prompt-driven~\citep{rafailov2023direct}. Eighth, we study zero-shot
general-purpose instruct models~\citep{zheng2023judging}; fine-tuning for
evaluation may align score distributions with human ratings~\citep{ke2019automated},
but whether it eliminates or merely attenuates the bias, and at what
cost to generality, remains open.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
% ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Score compression affects high-stakes LLM deployment in essay grading,
recruitment screening, and content moderation~\citep{zheng2023judging}.
Naive thresholds are miscalibrated (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and
\ref{sec:calibration}): requiring 9 for a scholarship or 8 for a resume
screen can reject qualified candidates through ceiling compression
(Table~\ref{tab:dist}), while floor compression can shield poor
submissions from remediation.

Inter-model bias compounds this (Section~\ref{sec:intermodel}):
Gemini~3~Flash scores identical texts about one point higher than
GPT-5~mini (Figure~\ref{fig:paireddiff}; Table~\ref{tab:compression}).
Switching or mixing LLM evaluators can create systematic, opaque
inequities~\citep{guilford1954psychometric}. Post-hoc mitigations
(Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}) only partly recover calibration and cannot
retrieve signals the model missed (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). LLM
scores are at best ordinal~\citep{stevens1971issues}; automated decisions
require context-specific validation against domain-expert baselines~\citep{ke2019automated}.

\section{Mechanistic Background Moved from Related Work}
\label{app:rlhf_mechanism}

Alignment training offers a plausible score-compression
mechanism~\citep{rafailov2023direct}. In RLHF, the policy-gradient
objective includes a KL term that keeps the fine-tuned policy
$\pi_\theta$ near the reference distribution $\pi_\text{ref}$~\citep{rafailov2023direct}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,
    \text{KL}(\pi_\theta(y|x)\,\|\,\pi_\text{ref}(y|x))
    \label{eq:rlhf}
\end{equation}
This regulariser penalises high-variance outputs and discourages
extreme predictions far from the training mass~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
Because the reverse KL used in standard RLHF is mode-seeking, it
concentrates probability on likely reference-policy outputs and can
amplify any central tendency in $\pi_\text{ref}$~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
Since human preference datasets themselves rarely contain extreme
ratings~\citep{stevens1971issues}, the interior of the scale becomes the
safe aligned region. \citet{rafailov2023direct} show that Direct
Preference Optimization (DPO) implicitly reparameterises this objective;
recent analysis suggests DPO can also contribute to distribution
compression through mode-seeking behaviour~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
This logit-level effect can compound RLHF distributional regularisation,
suppressing probability mass on extreme outputs~\citep{rafailov2023direct}.
Whether it is the primary cause is open; our design documents the
phenomenon rather than isolating mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:design}).

\subsection{Secondary Diagnostics}
\label{app:secondary_diagnostics}

Secondary diagnostics confirm that score--degradation relationships
are strictly monotonic but mildly concave, peaking in sensitivity at
low degradation (Figure~\ref{fig:doseresponse}). Score variance
exhibits a heteroscedastic ``fan-out-then-in'' pattern---peaking at
intermediate degradation levels (Figure~\ref{fig:boxplots};
Figure~\ref{fig:violins})---consistent with the hard ceiling and
floor compression effects described above. Detailed non-linearity
$F$-tests (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:nonlin}), Kendall's $\tau$ rank
correlations (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:tau}), and variance diagnostics
(Figure~\ref{fig:residuals}) are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}.



\subsection{Mixed-Effects Model}
\label{app:mixedeffects_details}

The next two subsections focus on GPT-5~mini and Gemini~3~Flash---the
two highest-performing frontier models---selected because they
represent distinct API providers and both achieve CR $> 0.60$ and
Spearman $\rho > 0.70$ (Table~\ref{tab:compression}). The three-way
specification requires exactly two model levels; inter-model agreement
metrics are inherently pairwise.

The model
\begin{equation}
    S_{ijk} \sim \lambda \times \mathrm{Axis} \times \mathrm{Model} +
    (1 + \lambda \mid \text{Article})
\end{equation}
converged with random intercepts and slopes. Key findings (full
coefficients in Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:mixedeffects_app}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item Degradation level: large negative main effect ($\beta =
    -4.76$, $z = -45.6$, $p < 10^{-300}$).
    \item Model intercept (Gemini vs.\ GPT): non-significant ($\beta =
    +0.07$, $p = 0.25$), indicating comparable baseline scoring at
    $\lambda = 0$. The significant $+1$ point median offset
    (Section~\ref{sec:intermodel}) emerges from interaction terms as
    degradation increases.
    \item All axis main effects significant; grammar shows the largest
    intercept reduction ($\beta = -1.49$, $z = -25.1$).
    \item All three-way $\mathrm{Level} \times \mathrm{Axis} \times
    \mathrm{Model}$ interactions significant. Largest: $\mathrm{Level}
    \times \mathrm{Lexical} \times \mathrm{Gemini}$ ($\beta = +1.93$,
    $z = +11.2$), confirming Gemini is substantially less sensitive to
    lexical degradation (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}).
    \item Random effects: article-level intercept variance $= 0.54$;
    slope variance $= 0.49$ (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:mixedeffects_app}).
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Intra-Condition Reliability}

ICC(1,1) across three repetitions per condition indicates high
consistency across all eleven models
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app}). GPT-5~mini (ICC $= 0.926$) and
Gemini~3~Flash (ICC $= 0.905$) are highest; ten of eleven models
achieve ICC $> 0.70$. The exception is MiniMax~M2P1 (ICC $= 0.433$).
Grammar yields the highest reliability for the top four models;
Information or Lexical degradation is most reliable for the rest
(Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}; Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app}).

\subsection{Effect Sizes}

Within-model effect sizes for the undegraded-vs.-maximally-degraded
contrast are extremely large: Cliff's $\delta = 0.986$ (GPT) and
$0.984$ (Gemini), with Cohen's $d = 4.65$ and $5.33$
(Table~\ref{tab:dist}; Table~\ref{tab:compression}). Between-model
effect sizes (Gemini $-$ GPT) peak at $\lambda = 0.2$ (Cohen's $d =
0.81$, rank-biserial $r = 0.79$; Figure~\ref{fig:scatter};
Figure~\ref{fig:paireddiff}).

\subsection{Covariates and Confounds}

\textbf{Article length.} A z-scored length covariate is
non-significant for GPT-5~mini ($\beta = -0.058$, $p = 0.21$) but
small and significant for Gemini~3~Flash ($\beta = -0.151$, $p =
0.002$), with negligible effect size. This is consistent with prior
work noting that verbosity signals can modulate LLM evaluation
outputs~\citep{zheng2023judging}.

\textbf{Category effects.} Kruskal-Wallis tests on undegraded scores
reveal significant category differences for both models (GPT: $H =
202.6$, $p < 10^{-42}$; Gemini: $H = 377.1$, $p < 10^{-80}$). Both
models score Science \& Technology highest (GPT: 8.44; Gemini: 8.73)
and Politics \& Society lowest (GPT: 7.58; Gemini: 7.88), despite all
articles being expert-authored pieces of comparable editorial quality
(Figure~\ref{fig:category}; Figure~\ref{fig:undegraded}).

\subsection{Multiple Testing Correction}

Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction across all 49 $p$-values leaves 47
significant at $\alpha = 0.05$. The two exceptions are the
article-length covariate for GPT-5~mini ($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.21$;
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary_diagnostics}) and Mistral~7B's lexical slope
($p_{\text{adj}} = 0.49$; Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:slopes_app}).



\section{Full Scaling and Generalization Analysis}
\label{app:multimodel_full}

Models span 3.8B (Phi-4~mini) to 228B (MiniMax~M2P1) parameters and use
the identical minimal prompt (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\subsection{Compression Across Models}

CRs range from 0.026 (Mistral~7B) to 0.685 (GPT-5~mini)
(Table~\ref{tab:compression}). Sub-10B models all have CR $<0.30$;
models above 100B all have CR $\geq0.42$ (Table~\ref{tab:compression}).

\subsection{Relationship Between Model Size and Compression}

Among nine open-weight models with known sizes, $\log_{10}(\text{parameters})$
correlates positively with CR (Pearson $r=0.78$, $p=0.013$;
Table~\ref{tab:compression}). The trend is noisy ($n=9$) and
non-monotonic: MiniMax~M2P1 (228B) has lower CR (0.42) than Qwen~2.5~14B
(0.45), and Gemma~2~9B reaches Spearman $\rho=0.70$ despite CR 0.30.
Thus size alone does not determine compression; architecture, training
data, and RLHF procedures~\citep{rafailov2023direct} likely contribute.
We treat this as a trend, not a scaling law.



\section{Additional Discussion Details}
\label{app:discussion_details}

\subsection{Linearity as Approximation}

The linear reference $S=10(1-\lambda)$ is interpretable despite concave
dose-response curves (Figure~\ref{fig:doseresponse}). Boundary absence is
model-free (Table~\ref{tab:dist}); affine, sigmoid, and isotonic
calibrations converge to similar curves (Section~\ref{sec:calibration},
Figure~\ref{fig:calibrecovery}); and if true perceptual quality is
concave, our CRs understate compression, making the benchmark
conservative.



\subsection{Reliability}

Both API models have ICC $>0.90$, and ten of eleven models exceed 0.70
(Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app}). Compression is therefore a stable
property of the scoring function, not noise, and GPT-5~mini's fixed
sampling behaviour does not add material variance relative to
temperature-0.0 models (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:icc_app}).



\subsection{Relationship to Prior Work on Calibration}

Supervised AES studies show task-specific fine-tuned models can match
human score distributions~\citep{ke2019automated}; rubric or binary-like
LLM evaluation reports smaller gaps between absolute scoring and relative
ranking~\citep{liu2023geval}. These results are compatible with ours
because they use specialist models or constrained scales requiring
supervision or prompt engineering~\citep{ke2019automated,liu2023geval}.
We study intrinsic zero-shot calibration of general-purpose instruct
models, the common LLM-as-judge setting~\citep{zheng2023judging}. That
compression can be reduced under ideal conditions~\citep{ke2019automated}
makes its persistence in uncalibrated deployment more important.




\section{Mitigation Formulas}
\label{app:mitigations}

\subsection{Strategy Definitions}

We evaluate five strategies targeting different aspects of compression;
the corresponding formulas are given below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quantile Normalization:} maps the empirical
    distribution to Uniform$[0,10]$ or Beta$(2,2)$ by quantile
    matching~\citep{bolstad2003comparison}, preserving rank order.
    \item \textbf{Expected-Score Rescaling:} replaces argmax with the
    probability-weighted expected score $\mathbb{E}[S]$~\citep{liu2023geval}.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetry Correction:} adjusts $\mathbb{E}[S]$ by a
    skewness penalty~\citep{liu2023geval}; cross-validation selects
    $\alpha=0.0$, reducing it to expected-score rescaling.
    \item \textbf{Auxiliary Regression:} trains supervised regressors on
    logprob-derived features to predict proxy ground truth.
    \item \textbf{Contrastive Anchor Prompting:} prepends explicit scale
    anchors (\eg ``0 = unreadable garbage'') to the prompt~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023geval}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Formula Details}


\textbf{Expected-Score Rescaling.} For models returning token-level
log-probabilities, the argmax score is replaced by the
probability-weighted expected value~\citep{liu2023geval}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{S} = \mathbb{E}[S] = \sum_{k=0}^{10} k \cdot p(k)
\end{equation}
where $p(k)$ is the softmax probability of score token $k$.

\textbf{Asymmetry Correction.} An extension of expected-score
rescaling~\citep{liu2023geval} correcting for distribution
skewness~\citep{stevens1971issues}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{S}_{\text{adj}} = \mathbb{E}[S] - \alpha \cdot
    \text{skewness}(p)
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ tuned via five-fold cross-validation. In our
experiments, the optimal $\alpha$ was 0.0 for all models, reducing
this method entirely to expected-score rescaling~\citep{liu2023geval}.
